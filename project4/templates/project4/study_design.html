Task 3 — The user study

A complete protocol for comparing the two elicitation interfaces. The study is designed, not run.

What is being compared, and the trap in it

The two designs are not equally expensive. A pairwise choice yields at most one bit and takes seconds. Ranking ten yields up to log2(10!) ≈ 21.8 bits but takes far longer and imposes real cognitive load. So "which elicits w better" is under-specified: better per what? Per interaction, ranking wins almost by definition. Per minute of a participant's time, it is a genuine question. Fixing the budget the comparison is normalised by is the central decision in this design.

Hypothesis

H1. For a fixed amount of participant time, ranking ten films yields a more accurate estimate of w than repeated pairwise choices.
H0. There is no difference in accuracy per unit of participant time.

A secondary hypothesis concerns cost rather than accuracy: ranking ten films is expected to impose greater perceived effort per trial than choosing between two, which is why effort is asked about directly at the end rather than inferred from the timings.

Participants and recruitment

  • Any adult who watches films. No domain expertise is needed, because the task asks about personal taste rather than knowledge — which also keeps the recruitment pool wide.
  • Recruited through university mailing lists and noticeboards, with the study run online so people take part at a time that suits them.
  • Compensated for their time. Asking for unpaid participation is both an ethical problem and a source of self-selection bias, since the people who volunteer for free are not a random sample.
  • Sample size taken from the pilot below rather than asserted.

Procedure, step by step

This is what would actually happen, in order. Every step below exists in the implemented interface.

  1. Informed consent. Nothing is recorded until it is given, and the study cannot be started without it.
  2. Instructions for the first interface, followed by practice trials so the first real trial is not also the first attempt.
  3. Block one: {{ trials_per_condition }} trials in the assigned first interface.
  4. A break, then instructions and practice for the second interface.
  5. Block two: {{ trials_per_condition }} trials in the other interface.
  6. Validation: {{ validation_trials }} further pairwise choices, never used to fit w.
  7. Questionnaire: perceived effort per interface, and which felt like a better way to describe their taste.
  8. Debrief: what was being compared, and the identifier needed to request erasure.

Before any of this runs for real, the study is piloted with five to ten participants, in person where possible so that hesitation and confusion can be observed rather than guessed at. Pilot data is excluded from the final analysis.

Design at a glance

TypeExperimental — the interface is manipulated, participants are randomly assigned
AssignmentWithin-subjects: every participant uses both interfaces
Why withinTaste varies enormously between people. Between-subjects would need far more participants to see the interface effect through that variance
Order controlCounterbalanced, alternating across participants, plus practice trials and a break between blocks
Trials{{ trials_per_condition }} per interface; ranking sets of {{ ranking_set_size }} films
Validation{{ validation_trials }} further pairwise choices at the end, never used to fit w. Without them the study could not compute its own primary measure
SelectionUniform at random from the dataset, as the brief permits

Measures

  • Primary. The share of the held-out choices that a preference vector, fitted from one interface alone, predicts correctly — divided by the minutes that interface consumed. Both interfaces are scored against the same held-out block, so they are compared on equal terms.
  • Secondary. Time per trial; remaining uncertainty about w; agreement of the resulting top-k recommendations.
  • Subjective. Perceived effort and preference between the two interfaces, from the closing questionnaire.

Ethics and data protection

  • Informed consent before anything is recorded; the study cannot be started without it.
  • Only a pseudonymous identifier is stored. No name, no email, no IP.
  • Withdrawal at any point, without giving a reason and without penalty.
  • Data minimisation: only responses and timings, because both are needed for the stated measures.
  • Right to erasure on request, and a route to exercise it.
  • Review by the university ethics board before recruitment.

Analysis plan

Exclusion criteria are fixed before collection: trials more than three standard deviations from the mean response time, participants who answer without variation, and anyone failing the attention check. Then descriptive statistics per condition, a paired test on the primary measure, and a correction for multiple comparisons across the secondary measures.

What the pilot says

Read agreement against the ceiling, not against 100%. A participant who is not perfectly consistent with themselves caps how well any preference vector can predict their held-out choices — including their own true one. The ceiling sits around 80%, so a design reaching 77% has recovered most of what was recoverable.

Ranking leads at every budget, but the shortest one barely. At two minutes a ranking block affords only two trials, too few to constrain {{ pilot.n_features }} weights, and its advantage nearly vanishes. The advantage grows once there is time for several rankings. That is a design finding: too short a session does not merely weaken the study, it erases the thing the study is trying to measure.

The effect is small, and the required sample is correspondingly large. Where ranking leads it leads by one to three percentage points, giving dz between 0.09 and 0.43. The sample sizes that follow range from about 45 to over a thousand, and that spread is itself informative: with an effect this small, even {{ pilot.n_participants }} simulated participants cannot pin the required n tightly. The defensible reading is that the study needs on the order of one to two hundred participants, not the twenty a coursework study would otherwise assume — and that quoting the most flattering row would be a mistake.
